# understand-argue

## Purpose

Makes the strongest honest case for a position, labelled one-sided.

## When to use

Use when the user wants the strongest honest case for one position, such as a pitch for a four-day week. Also use the light path when a response needs to reflect an existing view fairly.

## When not

- The case against a position: use the `understand-challenge` guide to find weak points.
- Choosing between positions or debating them: use the `understand-weigh` guide. A one-sided case does not choose a winner.
- The truth of a fact: use the `understand-research-fact` guide for evidence. A strong argument does not prove its facts.
- New options: use the `understand-brainstorm` guide to generate them.
- Writing the persuasive document itself: return it to the main task’s writing work. This guide supplies the case.

## Gives back

The case, labelled one-sided, in this default shape:

```text
Position:    stated precisely; if narrowed, from what and why
Argued for:  the goal, constraints and audience
Reasons:     strongest first, each with its evidence and strength, or marked as an assumption
Objections:  the strongest, each answered, conceded, or met by narrowing
Weaknesses:  what still stands against it
Strength:    strong, moderate or weak, from the evidence, and why
Next:        the evidence that would strengthen or break the case
One-sided:   the case for, not a verdict
```

A direct request gets the full shape. The `understand-weigh` guide uses each option's strongest reasons and weaknesses, and gets the challenge separately for reuse. A document-writing task turns the case into its argument and keeps weaknesses where readers need them. The light reflection path needs one or two faithful lines, not this shape. The skill that saves records gets the chosen option's case and its open weaknesses. The challenge's verdict is never part of the case.

## Rules

1. **Give the precise position through the `understand-frame` guide.** Give its claim, goal and constraints. A vague position can be defended against anything and proves nothing.
   - Get the audience from the caller or known context.
   - **If an unknown audience would change the case, ask through the `understand-clarify` guide.** Then end the turn.
   - If the unknown audience would not change the case, mark it unknown. Invent no audience concerns.
2. **Support each reason with evidence or mark it as an assumption.** Give its source, strength and limits. A reason from memory counts as unchecked until a source confirms it. State nothing false or overstated. False premises fail when relied on.
3. **Meet the strongest objections.** Answer each one with evidence, concede it, or narrow the position. Say which you did for each, and what you narrowed and why. Answering only weak objections is a strawman turned around.
   - Find the objections with the `understand-challenge` guide, or reuse a current challenge the caller gave you, by that guide's rule 7. A narrowed position or new evidence needs a new challenge.
   - Use the challenge's weaknesses as objections, and keep its verdict out of the case.
4. **Admit weaknesses.** Name objections still standing and thin reasons. Hidden weaknesses are found later at a worse time.
5. **Label the result one-sided.** Put one line beside the case, such as "This is the case for, not a verdict." The choice stays with the user or the `understand-weigh` guide. An unlabelled case reads as a recommendation.

For a faithful reflection of a view already in the conversation, by the main skill's step 6, use a light path: one or two lines that reflect it fairly, with no unsupported new claim. This is not an investigation or researched verdict. The full steps below serve a requested case or work needing evidence.

## Advice

- Reflect the user's view as they would put it. Check it with them when it matters.
- For the `understand-weigh` guide, give every option equal depth. Effort alone must not make one look stronger.

## Instructions

Build the strongest honest case for the requested position. Evidence, strong objections and admitted weaknesses let the reader judge the case without mistaking it for a verdict. **Define the position, support its reasons, meet objections, then return it labelled one-sided.**

If no honest case exists, show the best found and rate it weak. Invent nothing.

1. **Give the precise position through the `understand-frame` guide, by rule 1.** A vague claim can shift whenever challenged, leaving the caller with a case for something they did not ask for.
   - Give claim, goal and constraints. Get the audience from the caller or known context.
   - If an unknown audience would change the case, ask through the `understand-clarify` guide and end the turn. Otherwise mark it unknown and invent no concerns.
2. **Gather supported reasons under rule 2.** An unchecked premise can make a persuasive case fail as soon as someone relies on it.
   - Use the `understand-research-fact` guide for sourced factual checks and return the result here. Mark unchecked reasons as assumptions.
   - Give sources, strength and limits. Lead with a few strong reasons tied to the goal and known audience concerns. Check forecasts against how often such things succeed. A forecast built from this case alone tends to be too optimistic.
3. **Get the strongest objections through the `understand-challenge` guide, by rule 3.** Weak opposition makes the case look stronger without testing what a well-informed opponent would say.
   - Reuse a current challenge the caller gave you, by the `understand-challenge` guide's rule 7. A narrowed position or new evidence needs a new one.
4. **Answer, concede or narrow the position for each objection, by rule 3.** Leaving an objection unanswered hides a cost the reader may discover too late.
   - Check facts, argue interpretation and set rhetoric aside. Ask whether the position stands if the objection's checkable facts are true.
   - Explain any narrowing and why it was needed. Mention a stronger position beside the requested one rather than replacing it. The caller chooses which position to argue.
5. **Admit remaining weaknesses and rate the case, by rule 4.** A confident label without the open objections gives the caller no way to judge its limits.
   - Name thin reasons and objections still standing. Rate strong, moderate or weak from the evidence, with why and what could strengthen or break it.
6. **Check the case and “Gives back” before returning it.** Always do this last. A one-sided case must remain honest about its evidence and purpose.
   - Check fidelity, any narrowing, goal, constraints, audience, objections met, open weaknesses, strength and next evidence. Fix omissions first.
   - Check each claim in the case, answers to objections included, has its evidence or an assumption label, and says no more than its source, by rule 2.
   - Keep the one-sided line in the answer, and leave the choice to the user or the `understand-weigh` guide. Keep the challenge's verdict out of the case.
   - Apply the main skill's step 1 rules and the caller's needed result shape. A light reflection needs fidelity and no unsupported new claim, rather than a new investigation.
